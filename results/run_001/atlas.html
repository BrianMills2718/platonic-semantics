Do models trained separately end up organising meaning the same way?

Three language models, built by three different labs, that share no code and no vocabulary. Show each of them the same 212 concepts in English and in Chinese, and ask whether they arrange those concepts into the same shape — and whether relations like IsA or PartOf behave like a move you can reuse inside that shape.

Do the models organise concepts alike?
Partly

14 of 15 pairs beat chance. But roughly a third of that vanishes once you stop letting the model pick its own most-flattering layer, and cross-language agreement barely clears a 2017 static baseline.

Are relations reusable operations?
No, not per pair

Not one of the ten relations lets you take a specific pair and carry it to another model. Seven of ten do share a group-level direction — the average points the same way everywhere, the individual instances do not.

Is the measuring instrument sound?
One of six is broken

BLOOM's English geometry scores 0.156 for agreeing with itself when you reword the prompt. It sits inside 5 of the 15 pairs behind the headline.

How to read this

Everything here is measured against chance

Two models will always agree somewhat, for boring reasons — they read overlapping text, they spell words the same way. So every number on this page is compared against a null: the same computation run on scrambled data, a thousand times. The question is never "is the number big" but "is the number bigger than the scrambled version".

rho is agreement, from 0 (nothing) to 1 (identical).   q is how likely a result this strong is to be a fluke, already adjusted for the number of things tested — below 0.05 is the usual bar.   Held-out means the concepts used to make the choices were set aside before scoring, so nothing was tuned on what it was then graded on.

The section order below is not editorial. It is the reporting order this project fixed in writing before any real model had been run, so the most interesting-looking picture could not be promoted ahead of the load-bearing statistics. The 2-D map comes last for the same reason.

1 of 6 in the fixed reporting order — shared geometry

The models do share a concept layout, and it is not just spelling

The comparison cannot be made on the numbers inside each model, which have different sizes and no common coordinates. Instead each model is asked only about relative distances: is dog nearer wolf than to hammer? That question has the same answer shape in every model, so the answers can be lined up.

Two trivial explanations were tested and rejected. Removing surface spelling structure — word length, how the tokeniser splits it, frequency — costs about 5% of the effect. Removing everything a 2017 static word-embedding model already knows costs about 11%, and all 15 pairs stay significant. There is genuinely something that only the transformers share.

The deflating part. Split those pairs by language and the exciting half is the weak half. Two models reading the same language agree well above anything static. Two models reading different languages sit barely above what aligned static vectors already manage between the same two languages — and that is the comparison closest to "a language-independent space", the most quotable claim available.

3 of 6 in the fixed reporting order — relations, withdrawn and replaced

A relation has a shared direction, but you cannot carry one pair across

This is where the project's original result was wrong, and where the corrected one is more interesting. The idea was that IsA should behave like a move: whatever takes dog to mammal should also take hammer to tool. If that move is real and shared, you should find it in every model.

Both columns below are scored against the same fair comparison: two arbitrary concepts held the same distance apart. Without that control, close pairs and distant pairs are not comparable, and the whole test measures distance instead of meaning.

Read the left column first. Nothing is green. IsA, Causes and AtLocation are actually worse than arbitrary concept pairs: models agree less about a labelled relation than about two words picked at random the same distance apart. Whatever a relation is here, it is not a portable per-instance operation.

Then the right. Seven of ten relations show a real shared direction, all at q = 0.0014. HasProperty and UsedFor lead by a wide margin — both have narrow, repetitive target vocabularies, which is a warning as much as a result. The three that fail (Causes, Associated, SimilarTo) are exactly the three whose members are least alike as a class.

So the operator exists as an average and not as an instance. That is a narrower claim than the project set out to make, and the first honest one it has.

Instrument check

How much agreement was even reachable?

An agreement score of 0.27 means nothing on its own. It could be 0.27 out of a possible 0.30, which would be near-perfect, or 0.27 out of a possible 0.95, which would be feeble. Nothing in the original write-up distinguished those.

The cheap way to find out: extract every concept twice from the same model — once as a bare word, once averaged over six sentence templates — and see how much of its own layout survives the rewording. A model that disagrees with itself cannot agree with anyone else.

BLOOM's English system is not a usable instrument

It scores 0.156 against itself, and it agrees with Qwen's English system more than it agrees with itself — which voids that comparison rather than flattering it. It appears in 5 of the 15 pairs behind the headline number. This matches, from a completely independent direction, the earlier observation that its space is nearly collapsed.

Over the pairs where both systems are trustworthy, the models reach 0.468 of what was achievable: 0.615 when reading the same language, 0.394 across languages. Same direction as the static baseline finding, now with a scale attached. XGLM has no second extraction yet, so it is absent here.

Correction — 2026-09-04

What was withdrawn, and the two wrong turns after it

Run 001 originally reported that one relation, IsA, survived its null. That is withdrawn. Three different statistics were tried for this question and two of them were wrong; each failure is now a test in the repository rather than a paragraph.

withdrawn

Average the pairs, then compare

Averaging the pairs before comparing them cancels the pairing out entirely — the arithmetic reduces to "targets minus sources", so which source goes with which target makes no difference. Shuffling the pairing reproduces the published number to eight decimal places. It was measuring agreement about a centroid direction, under the name of a transformation.

rejected

Compare each pair to its own counterpart

Fixed the pairing problem and passed all ten relations at once, which was the tell. Random non-relational word pairs passed it too, scoring higher than any real relation. It was re-measuring the shared geometry from section 1, one pair at a time. Pair distance was driving all of it.

rejected

Subtract how alike the relation's own pairs are

Penalised the very thing that makes a relation a relation. A coherent relation was scored below scattered random pairs, and every single relation came out negative.

shipped

Report both quantities against distance-matched pairs

Keep "can you carry a pair across" and "do the pairs point the same way" separate, and compare each to arbitrary concepts held the same distance apart. This is the table above.

6 of 6 in the fixed reporting order — last, deliberately

The map, which is the least trustworthy thing here

Flattening this to two dimensions keeps only 14.8% of the real structure. It is included because people ask for it, and placed last because the project's own rules say a pretty projection is not a success criterion. Colour is how stably a concept keeps its neighbours across all six systems — that, not the position, is the measured quantity.

Every figure on this page is read from the run's own output files — results/run_001/primary/ and results/run_001/reanalysis_20260904/. Nothing here is illustrative. The synthetic mock this replaces is still at prototypes/semantic_space_explorer.html and remains synthetic.

Known limits, stated plainly: 0.5B-parameter models, bare single words, one prompt template for the primary run, one split seed. The 15 system pairs are not independent. Per-relation sample size is 14–18. 2.8% of the Chinese benchmark is unknown to XGLM's tokeniser. The static baseline is Wikipedia-trained, as these models partly are, so it is a non-transformer baseline rather than an independent one.
