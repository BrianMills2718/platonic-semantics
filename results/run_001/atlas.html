Do minds built separately end up organising meaning the same way?

Three language models from three different labs, sharing no code and no vocabulary, each shown the same 212 concepts in English and in Chinese.

Where the map below gets its distances. Neuron 5 in Qwen has nothing to do with neuron 5 in BLOOM, and an 896-dimensional space cannot be laid over a 1024-dimensional one, so no model's own coordinates were used, or could be. The one thing that survives the crossing is which concepts each system puts near which, and the map is built from that alone: concepts are pulled together by how many systems agree they belong together. Distance on it is agreement, measured — not a projection of anyone's internal space.

The atlas

Where a shared semantic space resolves, and where it dissolves

Each of the six systems was asked independently for every concept's ten nearest neighbours. Two concepts are linked here when systems agree they belong together, and the link is thicker the more systems agree. Nothing was projected down from any model's own coordinates — the layout is pulled into shape by those links alone, so a short distance on this map means the models concur, which is the one thing that does survive crossing between them.

A concept is drawn as sharp as the six views agree it is. Where all six put a word in the same company it resolves to a hard point. Where they scatter, there is no single place to put it, and it is drawn as the smear it actually is. The fog is not a rendering effect — it is the measurement.

Now raise the demand. The slider sets how many of the six systems must agree before a link between two concepts is worth drawing. At 1 the web has 2,124 links and nothing is being asked. At 6 — unanimous — 130 survive. Ninety-four per cent of the apparent agreement was never shared by all six minds, and 88 of the 212 concepts lose every connection they had.

Watch where it survives. Links only two systems endorse are within a semantic region 43% of the time and run a mean distance of 0.42 across the map. Unanimous links are within a region 76% of the time at a mean distance of 0.20 — twice as local, and half as long. dog–wolf, dog–cat, animal–plant, family–school hold unanimously; the long links tying one region to another are the first to go.

That is the shape of the result. All six minds largely agree about the neighbourhoods. Far fewer agree about how the neighbourhoods fit together. If there is a shared semantic space in this run, it is found in patches rather than as a whole — which is also why the relation tests further down come back the way they do.

Try child, then hammer. Human-role words are the most stable things in the benchmark — child, student, teacher, father all hold their neighbours across every system. Ordinary physical objects mostly do not. That split was not designed for and nobody predicted it.

Some instability has a known cause worth checking before reading anything into it: mammal and whale are among six Chinese terms that XGLM's tokeniser does not know, so in that one system they are indistinguishable from each other.

Where it stands

Three questions, three answers

Do the models organise concepts alike?
Partly

14 of 15 pairs beat chance. A third of that vanishes once models stop picking their own most flattering layer, and the cross-language half barely clears a 2017 baseline.

Are relations reusable moves?
Only on average

No relation lets you carry a specific pair to another model. Seven of ten share a group-level direction. The move exists as an average, not as an instance.

Is the instrument sound?
One of six is broken

BLOOM's English geometry scores 0.156 for agreeing with itself when the prompt is reworded, and sits inside 5 of the 15 pairs.

Level one — is the arrangement shared?

Same language, yes. Across languages, barely.

The gap between the middle bar and the bottom one is the entire cross-language claim — and the bottom bar is a static word-embedding model from 2017 with no transformer in it. The most quotable version of this project's thesis is the least supported part of it.

Level two — are relations objects in their own right?

The average points somewhere. The instances do not.

Instrument check

How much agreement was even reachable?

A score of 0.27 means nothing alone: 0.27 out of a possible 0.30 is near-perfect, 0.27 out of 0.95 is feeble. Extract every concept twice from the same model — bare word, then averaged over six sentence templates — and see how much of its own layout survives the rewording. A model that disagrees with itself cannot agree with anyone.

BLOOM's English system is not a usable instrument

It scores 0.156 against itself, and agrees with Qwen's English system more than it agrees with itself — which voids that comparison rather than flattering it. Over the pairs where both systems are trustworthy, the models reach 0.468 of what was achievable: 0.615 within a language, 0.394 across.

Correction — 2026-09-04

What was withdrawn, and the two wrong turns after it

Run 001 originally reported that one relation, IsA, survived its null. That is withdrawn. Three statistics were tried and two were wrong; each failure is now a test in the repository rather than a paragraph.

withdrawn

Average the pairs, then compare

Averaging before comparing cancels the pairing out entirely — the arithmetic reduces to "targets minus sources", so which source goes with which target makes no difference. Shuffling the pairing reproduces the published number to eight decimals.

rejected

Compare each pair to its own counterpart

Passed all ten relations at once, which was the tell. Random non-relational word pairs passed it too, scoring higher than any real relation. It was re-measuring the shared arrangement from level one, one pair at a time.

rejected

Subtract how alike the relation's own pairs are

Penalised the very thing that makes a relation a relation, scoring a coherent relation below scattered random pairs. Every relation came out negative.

shipped

Report both quantities against distance-matched pairs

Keep "can you carry a pair across" and "do the pairs point the same way" separate, and compare each to arbitrary concepts held the same distance apart.

What the map is and is not. An earlier version of this page ended with a scatterplot of a projected consensus space. That was removed rather than softened: projecting invents a coordinate frame the project defines as non-existent, and flattening the data that way keeps only 14.8% of the structure. The map at the top is built the other way round, from the agreement links themselves, and those links are drawn — so what you read is the structure, not a position standing in for it. Layout is a convenience; the edges are the data. As a check that the layout is not noise: concepts from the same semantic category land at mean distance 0.33, where unrelated ones land at 0.73.
